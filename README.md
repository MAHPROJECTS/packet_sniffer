NetScope is a desktop app that lets you see and understand what’s on your local network, using two techniques in one window. The first is active device discovery: when you press Scan, it broadcasts an ARP request across your subnet (auto-detected as a /24, or whatever you type in) and every live device answers with its IP and MAC address. A pool of 20 background threads then profiles each device in parallel. It looks up the manufacturer from the MAC prefix, resolves a hostname via reverse DNS with a NetBIOS fallback, and tries a TCP connection to a list of common ports (SSH, HTTP, SMB, printers, Chromecasts and so on) to see which are open. It reads any banner a service offers, and sends a single ping to read the TTL and make a rough Linux-vs-Windows guess. The second technique is passive packet capture: when you press Start, scapy’s AsyncSniffer listens on your chosen interface, optionally restricted by a BPF filter, and every packet it sees is parsed into its protocol (TCP, UDP, ICMP, ARP, DNS), its source and destination, a scope label (your own traffic, broadcast/multicast, or another device’s), and a readable summary of the ports, flags, DNS queries and answers, or payload text. DNS responses are also remembered so later packets can show hostnames next to IPs. Because tkinter can’t safely be touched from background threads, both the scanner and the sniffer put their results into a queue, and a function called _poll running every 50 milliseconds on the main thread drains the queue and updates the screen. That keeps the window responsive even under heavy traffic. The interface is split into three tabs: Devices shows the discovered machines with a details pane and lets you jump straight to capturing one device’s traffic, Live Capture shows a colour-coded packet table with a layer-by-layer and hex-dump view of any packet you select, plus pause, clear and pcap save and export options, and Statistics summarises everything with a protocol bar chart, top talkers by bytes, and a protocol-by-scope breakdown. Underneath it all, the logic of your two original scripts is intact, and the new code just wraps it in a GUI and moves the slow work onto threads.
